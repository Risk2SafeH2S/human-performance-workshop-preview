## The Method

1. **Screen first, in the Select (concept) phase, on every project of any size.** Run the 11-question preliminary screen as a group. One "yes" means HFE must be considered; no "yes" means record that and stop.
2. **Detailed screen** once scope and design detail are known, with O&M people and at least one HFE-competent person. Decide: the HFE inputs and activities, the level of specialist input (low, medium, high), the strategy, the specific HF risks, and whether an HF integration plan (HFIP) is needed.
3. **Size the input.** Low: simple tasks, off-the-shelf or like-for-like, covered by existing standards. Medium: complex or lengthy tasks or high reliance on human reliability, real scope to influence design, or a goal-setting duty to show HFE was applied. High: medium plus mandated HFE-led work, specific studies (control room, HMI), safety critical tasks or major accident potential.
4. **Decide on the plan.** Low: no HFIP, record the strategy in the project safety plan. Medium: HFIP recommended, can be a section of the safety plan. High: standalone HFIP.
5. **On high-input projects, run the equipment screening tool**: a facilitated workshop rating each unit, item or operation on six factors (task complexity, unit criticality, task frequency, novelty, design scope, known problems), then deciding which items need further HFE work.
6. **Pick activities by project type** from the look-up: design specifications, awareness training, task requirements analysis (TRA), valve criticality analysis (VCA), vendor package screening, control room analysis, HMI analysis, alarm analysis, layout review, HF input to HAZOP, human error assessment and safety critical task analysis (SCTA).
7. **Assign roles**: HFE lead (HF integration manager on big jobs), practitioners who do the analyses, support staff who apply standards. Add a coordinator, a technical authority for deviations, and an HF working group as the project grows.
8. **Write the HFIP** and keep it live: roles and interfaces, standards, methods and end-user involvement, issue management and trade-offs, activities with inputs, outputs and acceptance criteria, programme, upkeep of the plan.
9. **Set up one issue register (HFIR)** at the start; every activity feeds it.
10. **Define (FEED):** settle standards, write HFE design specifications, give awareness training, start the analyses, review at the 30% model, put HF into hazard studies, track issues.
11. **Execute, detailed design:** continue analyses, review at 60% and 90% models, vendor and valve compliance, HMI prototypes; close all issues; issue the HFE close-out report and the HFE plan for construction.
12. **Execute, construction and commissioning:** awareness training for construction staff, checklist verification that as-built matches the design, pre-start-up audits, final close-out, lessons learned.
13. **Operate:** follow-up evaluation about a year after start-up; feed findings back.

## Rules

- Sort each requirement as prescriptive (check by design review), goal-oriented (convert to specific requirements by analysis) or process (a named activity in the plan).
- National regulation and company requirements come first.
- Like-for-like still gets reviewed, at a depth set by criticality. "Proven" means designed with HF principles and accepted by users with no incidents or complaints.
- Acceptance criteria are a mix of compliance with standards, positive reviews and trials, and end-user acceptance; minor projects can use compliance alone.
- Competence: Level 1 support (awareness training); Level 2 practitioner (relevant degree, professional membership, 1 year in high-hazard industry); Level 3 lead (certified professional, 5+ years in high-hazard industry with at least 2 in energy).
- Valves: Category 1 (critical or frequent) gets permanent access at grade or a permanent platform with identity and status visible; Category 2 ideally deck level or stairs; Category 3 may use mobile platforms or scaffolding, never portable ladders.
- Vendor packages: Category 1 (critical or frequent manual intervention) gets specialist review as installed on the plant; Category 2 only needs the vendor's standards and anthropometric basis.
- Model reviews: 30% is the key one for space, clearances, walkways and stairs; 60% checks reach to valves and controls; 90% verifies closure.
- HAZOP: at least one attendee with Level 1 competence responsible for HF; Level 2 or above where there is significant human interaction.
- A task is safety critical if a human failure could cause a major accident, escalate one, weaken a barrier or impair recovery.
- HFE issues carry equal weight with other design issues; trade-offs are documented, and deviations go to the technical authority.

## Examples

- Wrong reactor among 24 identical ones, interlock overridden: make identity and status obvious, detect bypasses, do not rely on a procedure alone.
- Three dimensionally identical elbows, wrong metal fitted: design so incompatible parts cannot be swapped.
- Drain valve hidden under insulation left open after a turnaround: visibility and access to drains and plugs.
- HAZOP guidewords for HF: operational barriers, maintenance barriers, escalation factors.

## Mistakes To Avoid

- Starting HFE late; skipping the 30% review.
- Skipping review because a design is "not new".
- Burying HFE issues untagged in a general tracker.
- Closing hazards with training and procedures when an engineered change is needed.
- Reviewing a vendor skid in isolation; assuming off-the-shelf means no influence.
- Running TRA on everything, or expecting specialists to check every valve.
- Screening without operators and maintainers.
- Generic checklists or untrained checkers during construction; letting field-run items erode clearances.
- Skipping the one-year follow-up.

## The Method

This part is front matter plus sections 1 and 2. It sets up the method and gives the map of the whole report; the detailed steps come later. What it establishes:

1. Overall flow of the report, which is the flow of HFE on a project (pp. 11-12, from the section descriptions and contents list):
   - Screen the project to decide how much HFE it needs (section 3): preliminary screening, then detailed screening; pick the HFE design inputs and activities; decide how much HF specialist input is needed; decide whether an HF integration plan (HFIP) is required; assign roles and responsibilities.
   - Plan the integration (section 4): write the HFIP; fix the HF standards and design specifications; set acceptance criteria; decide how compliance evidence will be recorded; set up issue logging and tracking, including an HF issues register (HFIR) and a way to handle constraints and trade-offs; set up end-user involvement.
   - Carry out the activities by project phase (section 5): Select, Define, Execute, Operate (post-start-up evaluation and HF during operations), Decommissioning; then the list of deliverables.
2. Sort every HFE requirement on the project into one of three types and treat each differently (pp. 14-15):
   - Prescriptive: fixed dimensions, distances, spaces, weights that a designer applies directly (workspace envelope, ladder dimensions, headroom over walkways). Method: agree a technical baseline, then check compliance through design reviews (isometric drawings, 3D model, control room layout, HMI graphics prototypes). Deviations go through change management.
   - Goal-oriented: states the outcome, not the design parameter (cut human error potential to ALARP; HMI graphics that support operator situational awareness). Sources: regulations, standards, project safety requirements, HAZOP and HAZID outputs. Method: run HFE analyses to convert each goal into specific technical requirements that can be designed in.
   - Process: names the activities that must be done (task requirements analysis, valve criticality analysis, 3D model design review). Whatever the HFIP lists is in effect a process requirement.
   Complex projects normally have all three.
3. Use the outcome as the test: following the approach should let the project show that HFE-related risk and human error potential were reduced to ALARP during design (p. 9).

Named analyses and tools listed in the contents (detail is in later parts): task requirements analysis (TRA, D.1); valve criticality analysis (VCA, D.2); vendor package screening and review (D.3); control room analysis and design review (D.4); HMI analysis and review (D.5); alarm systems analysis and review (D.6); facility/plant layout design review (D.7); HF input to HAZOP with HF guidewords (E.1); safety critical task analysis (SCTA, E.2); HFE equipment screening tool scored on task complexity, unit criticality, task frequency, novelty, design scope and known problems (A.3, Tables A.4-A.9); HFE plan for construction (Annex F); HFIP template (Annex C); competency framework and roles: HFE coordinator, HFE technical authority, HF working group (Annex B).

## Rules

- Every project, from a minor modification to a new facility, should have HF issues considered and its HFE requirements determined (p. 10).
- Apply HFE early; early phases of major projects matter most for buy-in (pp. 9, 15).
- Where national regulation or company requirements apply, they take priority, especially mandated activities (p. 10).
- Have a change management process to control derogations from prescriptive requirements, because trade-offs against other constraints are common (p. 15).
- Scope boundary: the guidance covers what an engineering project controls (design and layout of facilities and systems), concept design through construction/commissioning. Staffing, shift scheduling, organisation and supervision, competency management and safety culture are outside scope. Operations and decommissioning get only brief coverage (pp. 9-10).
- It gives the process, not ergonomic design data; look elsewhere for HMI or accessibility principles (p. 10).
- Definitions (pp. 13-14):
  - Human factors (HF): how job, individual and organisation shape behaviour at work affecting health and safety (HSE HSG48 view); the IEA frames it as the discipline of human-system interaction aimed at well-being and system performance. "Ergonomics" and "human factors" are interchangeable; ergonomics tends to mean the physical side.
  - Human factors engineering (HFE): applying HF knowledge to design and construction of socio-technical systems so the human contribution to production is optimised and design-induced risks to health, personal safety, process safety and environment are minimised. Mainly about the physical workplace: accessible, operable, maintainable equipment. Design to fit people, not the reverse.
  - HFI: human factors integration, the regulators' term for building HFE into projects.
  - HFIP: HF integration plan; also called HF implementation plan or HFE integration plan (HFEIP) — same thing (p. 11 footnote).
  - ALARP: as low as reasonably practicable.
- Regulatory drivers named (p. 14): UK offshore safety case regulations 2015 (evidence that HFE design risk is ALARP); UK COMAH onshore sites; Singapore major hazard installations regulations 2017; regulators in Norway and Australia; NORSOK S-002 working environment standard; company standards.
- Benefits to cite when making the case (p. 16): fewer errors and violations; lower project cost through right-first-time design and less rework; better task efficiency and lower operating and maintenance cost; fewer physical and mental health problems; better working conditions, lower absence and turnover.

## Examples

Three incident cases of design-induced error (pp. 16-19), each laid out as what happened, consequences, root causes, human performance, design issues, recommendations:

- Vinyl chloride monomer explosion (US CSB report 2004-10-I-IL). An operator went to the wrong reactor among 24 identical ones arranged in groups of four, found the bottom valve interlocked, and overrode the interlock with emergency air without asking permission. Five dead, three injured, community evacuated. Design lessons: make reactor identity and status obvious at the point of work; detect interlock bypass automatically; do not rely on a written procedure against a catastrophic hazard. Contributing: no radio link between levels, supervisor role removed, cleaning procedure never hazard-analysed, earlier similar incidents not acted on.
- Resid hydrotreater fire (CSB Safety Bulletin 2005-04-B, October 2006). Three 8-inch elbows were made dimensionally identical to save fabrication cost; a maintenance contractor swapped a carbon steel elbow into an alloy position. It failed after about three months from high temperature hydrogen attack. One minor injury, $30 million damage. Lessons: design so incompatible parts cannot be interchanged; treat verification of the right part as safety critical; consider the whole life cycle including maintenance.
- Propane release from a drain valve after a cat cracker turnaround. A drain valve on a level bridle was left open, hidden under insulation and hard to reach; the plug was only partly threaded. It blew out 20 hours after reaching 260 psig (17.9 barg), releasing 13,000-16,000 kg of C3. No ignition, no injuries, but high potential. Lessons: drain valve and plug visibility and access matter; direct visual confirmation beats indirect indicators.

Examples of each requirement type (Figure 2, p. 14): prescriptive — workspace envelope, ladder dimensions, headroom; goal-oriented — support the human error ALARP demonstration, support situational awareness; process — VCA, 3D model review, TRA.

## Mistakes To Avoid

- Leaning on a procedure as the only barrier for a high-consequence hazard.
- Many identical units or interchangeable parts with nothing to tell them apart.
- Interlock bypasses that can be used without permission or detection.
- Saving construction cost by standardising parts in a way that creates a maintenance error trap.
- Valves, drains and plugs that cannot be seen or reached; assuming hidden items are in the normal position.
- Ignoring combinations of minor factors that together make error likely; not learning from earlier incidents or hazard study actions.
- Bringing HFE in late, causing expensive rework.
- Treating this report as a source of ergonomic dimensions or as detailed how-to for each analysis; it is neither.

## The Method

Page numbers are the report's printed numbers (printed 20 to 39; the working file calls these PDF pages 21 to 40).

**A. Screen first (Section 3, pp. 20-25).** Screening is the first HFE task on any project, big or small, done in the Select (concept selection) phase. It has to answer five things: is HFE needed at all; how much; what HF-competent resource and roles; which HFE activities; which specific HF risks and opportunities the facility scope carries.

1. Preliminary screening (p. 21-22, question set in Annex A.1). A group exercise with a cross-section of people who know the project scope. A single "yes" to any screening question means HFE must be considered; no "yes" answers means record that no HFE requirement was found and stop.
2. Detailed screening (p. 22, Annex A.2/A.3). Run once scope is confirmed and design details are known; it also re-checks the preliminary findings. Group exercise with people who know the design scope, programme and O&M implications, plus at least one person competent in HFE. Outputs: (a) the HFE design inputs and activities, (b) level of HF specialist input (low/medium/high), (c) the HFE strategy, (d) specific HF risks for the equipment scope, (e) whether an HF integration plan (HFIP) is needed.
3. Large or complex projects split screening into these two passes; smaller ones may do one.
4. Flow of Figure 3 (p. 21): preliminary screening -> any yes? -> detailed screening -> pick inputs/activities from Table 1 -> estimate specialist input -> low = HFIP not required, medium = HFIP recommended, high = standalone HFIP required -> record the HFE strategy.

**B. Pick activities by project type (Table 1, pp. 23-24).** Indicative, not exhaustive:
- Complete new plant/facility: HFE design specifications; HFE awareness training; task requirements analysis (TRA); valve criticality analysis (VCA); vendor package screening and review; control room analysis; HMI analysis; alarm system analysis; plant layout review; HF input to safety/risk work (SCTA, HAZOP, HRA).
- New or significantly modified HMIs (incl. alarm changes): design specifications; TRA; HMI review; alarm review; HF input to safety/risk work.
- Layout changes affecting access to and operability of valves/equipment: design specifications; awareness training; TRA; VCA; layout review; HF input to safety/risk work.
- New-to-site or untested systems/equipment: awareness training; TRA; vendor package screening and review; HF input to safety/risk work.
- New control room or control facilities: design specifications; TRA; control room review; HMI review; alarm review; HF input to safety/risk work.
- Non-design HF work that may also be needed: procedures and work instructions, competency assurance, workload/staffing studies.

**C. Assign roles (3.6, pp. 25-26).** Three responsibility bands map to three roles (Table 2):
- Manage HFE work -> HFE lead, called HF integration manager (HFIM) on high-input projects. Owns coordination and integration with the project team and programme. May sit in the operating company or the design contractor; very large projects may have an HFIM in each party reporting to a senior HFIM. Must be an HF specialist at "HFE lead" competence.
- Perform HFE reviews and analyses -> HFE practitioner; an HF professional at "practitioner" level as a minimum.
- Apply HF/ergonomics standards only -> HFE support; non-HF staff acceptable on low-input projects, with awareness training recommended.

**D. Plan the integration (Section 4, pp. 27-34).** Write an HFE strategy whenever screening says HFE applies; write a formal HFIP for medium-to-high input. The HFIP must cover:
1. Roles, responsibilities, interfaces with other disciplines, and cross-organisation dependencies.
2. Which HF/ergonomics standards apply, including company ones.
3. Approaches and methods, including how end-users are involved.
4. How HFE issues are tracked, managed and resolved, including trade-offs.
5. Activities and work packages with inputs, outputs/deliverables and acceptance criteria.
6. How the work fits the project programme, with timescales and milestones.
7. How the plan itself is maintained.
Also: relationship to other plans (project safety plan, RAM plan), how compliance evidence is documented, required deliverables.

Timing and authorship (p. 28): written by a practitioner-level specialist at minimum, lead-level on complex projects. Figure 4 and Table 3 put strategy/HFIP production in Select; section 4.2 says the HFIP is produced early, at Define (FEED). It is a living document, revised for Execute (especially if FEED and EPC contractors differ) and whenever scope changes. Multi-organisation projects can use a main HFIP pointing to sub-HFIPs per party.

**E. Life-cycle sequence (Section 5, Figure 4 and Tables 3-4, pp. 35-39).** Phases used: Select (concept selection), Define (early design/FEED), Execute (detailed design, then construction and commissioning), Operate.
- Select: HFE screening; HFE strategy/HFIP; assign roles; set up the HF issues register (HFIR) or other issue mechanism, including an agreed route for deviations.
- Define: review proposed standards against the strategy and resolve conflicts; develop HFE design specifications; deliver awareness training; start the HFE design analyses; start design review and validation; give HF input to hazard identification/risk work; track and close issues.
- Execute (detailed design): continue analyses, reviews, risk input and issue tracking; produce the HFE close-out report and HFE plan for construction.
- Execute (construction/commissioning): awareness training again; pre-start-up audits; HFE close-out and lessons-learned review.
- Operate: follow-up evaluation.

Design analyses (p. 39) exist to pin down the O&M tasks the design must support and the end-user requirements for safe, effective performance, over and above standards. They generate extra requirements for project and procurement specifications, and issues for the HFIR. VCA, control room, HMI and alarm work usually start in Define and run into Execute. The loop is iterative: analysis -> recommendations -> design review with discipline engineers and end-users -> repeat.

## Rules

- Any single "yes" in preliminary screening triggers HFE consideration (Figure 3).
- Low input: no HFIP. Medium: HFIP recommended. High: standalone HFIP required (Figure 3; Annex A.2.2). A contract or company rule can require an HFIP regardless.
- Check the contract: it may mandate HFE input or name the activities.
- "Proven" design means previously designed with HF principles and accepted by end-users with no incidents or complaints. Like-for-like still gets reviewed, at a depth set by equipment/process criticality (p. 22).
- Minor modification with no new HMIs or equipment -> less specialist input; major modification, new facility or new systems -> more (p. 25).
- Workload assessment: usually unnecessary for modifications; for a new facility or major process change it may be needed to show staffing covers normal, abnormal and emergency scenarios. If done, specialist input is essential (p. 24).
- Standards not fixed by contract: consult an HF specialist, considering legislation, company rules, facility location and the target user population's body dimensions. The HFE lead resolves conflicts and sets priority order (p. 28).
- Project-specific HFE design specifications are worthwhile when guidance is scattered; typical topics are control rooms and workspaces, living quarters layout, access/egress routes, stairs and ladders, valve locations, labelling and signage. Bespoke specifications are needed for novel aspects with no standard. For HFE-critical equipment, put the specifications into vendor tender requirements (pp. 29, 38).
- Acceptance criteria (pp. 29-30) go in the HFIP, overall and per activity. Usually a mix of: demonstrated compliance with standards; positive results from reviews, tests and trials; end-user acceptance. Minor projects: compliance only. Where there are gate reviews, write criteria as pass/fail and consider specialist sign-off at the gate.
- Compliance evidence (p. 30): closed-out issues, plus as appropriate short compliance statements, per-activity reports, and a final close-out report. HFE lead decides the level.
- Issues (pp. 30-31): log, manage and track every one to resolution. A general project tracker is acceptable only if HFE items are tagged so the HFE lead can approve closure. Major projects should consider a standalone HFIR, set up at project start, with terms of reference in the HFIP: how it is populated, review frequency, circulation, closure mechanism (possibly technical authority sign-off). Record HFE issues in one place and cross-reference rather than duplicate. Issue the HFIR with the close-out report; carry open items to the next phase. HFE issues get equal weight with other design issues.
- Trade-offs (pp. 31-32) weigh cost against demonstrable risk reduction, criticality of the affected operation and organisational risk tolerance, and whether the requirement is statutory or mandated. The HFE lead (or competent deputy) facilitates documented trade-off meetings with discipline engineers and end-users; a separate HFE technical authority, where one exists, has final say on deviations.
- End-user involvement (p. 32) must be described in the HFIP: walk/talk-throughs for TRA, iterative design reviews, desktop reviews of concepts, mock-up and simulator trials, study of a "reference" existing situation, trade-off discussions. Complex projects may set up an HF working group (HFWG).
- Awareness training (pp. 34, 38): at minimum the design team and discipline engineers, early; contractors and vendors on complex projects; a process-focused version for senior managers. Focus it on the project's own standards and on when to call a specialist.
- A formal end-of-project lessons-learned review is required, covering design issues, trade-offs, and the integration process; incident lessons feed in too.

## Examples

- Legacy HMIs that fall short of good practice and cannot easily be changed: apply interim mitigations now and plan longer-term improvement (p. 31).
- Layout constraints on a modification that prevent full maintenance clearances: a typical trade-off case (p. 31).
- Early Define-phase review activities named: procurement support, vendor document review against specifications, 30 % 3D model review, concept layouts, HMI specifications, alarm philosophy (listed on the following page, p. 40).

## Mistakes To Avoid

- Skipping review because a design is "not new" or like-for-like.
- Starting HFE late; requirements must be set at the beginning (p. 33).
- Burying HFE issues untagged in a general tracker, or duplicating them across registers.
- Treating HFE constraints as lesser than other technical constraints.
- No budget, no sponsor approval, no senior commitment, no defined performance measures.
- Specialists located away from the design team.
- Letting the HFIP go stale after scope or contractor changes.

## The Method

**A. Rest of the Define phase (Table 4 continued, pp. 40-41).**
1. Design review and validation starts in Define and deepens through Execute. HF specialists work iteratively with discipline engineers and operators/maintainers until the requirements from the analyses are met. Early items: procurement support for vendor equipment, checking vendor documents against HFE specifications, 30 % 3D model reviews, concept layouts, HMI specifications, alarm philosophy.
2. HF input to hazard identification (HAZOP, HAZID, bowtie) so human error potential and consequences are examined. Then, on most projects, specialist input to show human-error risks are reduced to ALARP. Depth depends on screening results and how manual the tasks are.
   - Qualitative human error assessment (HEA): expected on most projects.
   - Safety critical task analysis (SCTA): expected where there is major accident potential; detailed HEA on the tasks judged safety critical.
   - Quantitative HEA giving human error probabilities (HEPs): optional, specialist-led.
3. Log every issue in the agreed register; the HFE lead makes sure actions and owners exist. Open items at the end of Define transfer to Execute.

**B. Execute, detailed design (Table 5, pp. 42-44).**
1. Continue Define analyses and add new ones, e.g. critical operations or maintenance tasks not covered deeply enough in FEED.
2. Review and validation: 60 % and 90 % 3D model reviews; vendor package isometric drawings; valve positioning compliance; HMI graphics screenshots, demos, prototypes. Specialists confirm all HFE requirements are met, flag non-compliance, and agree solutions (with trade-offs) alongside designers and end-users. For major accident hazard projects this includes confirming error-reducing design features are in. The outputs form part of the ALARP demonstration.
3. SCTA/HEA ideally done in Define; revisit if O&M task details are still settling.
4. All HFE issues should be closed by the end of detailed design, though some verification may remain for construction.
5. HFE close-out report at the end of detailed design. Minimum on any project with HFE input: record the inputs and activities and the evidence of compliance (or where to find it). Major projects: a formal report summarising activity outputs per the HFIP, showing how every issue closed, with the final HFIR attached or the project tracker cross-referenced. Very large projects with a different FEED contractor may also issue one at the end of Define.
6. HFE plan for construction, written at the end of detailed design, covering verification and validation during construction and commissioning: awareness training for construction staff, issues to check on walk-arounds and any checklists, and a schedule of audits and reviews. Standalone or folded into the close-out report; that choice is made in Select and recorded in the HFIP.

**C. Execute, construction and commissioning (pp. 44-46).**
1. Aim: equipment installed as agreed, with no erosion of clearances, sightlines and similar, and outstanding issues resolved.
2. Awareness training for construction, inspection and commissioning personnel, delivered by HF specialists and built from the detailed-design close-out report and carried-forward actions. Goal is an operability-and-maintainability mindset, e.g. understanding the trouble that wrong installation causes later.
3. Verification and validation: check flagged issues are fixed and "as built" matches the agreed design. Usually acceptable for trained non-specialists using specialist-written checklists. Checklists must be project-specific and point at the most critical items: operability, maintainability, access, egress, safety. Small operator-led modifications may have specialists audit directly.
4. Pre-start-up audits: specialist involvement where high HFE-related risk was identified; state this in the HFIP.
5. Final close-out review before start-up (complexity-dependent) confirming: all issues closed; verification reviews and pre-start-up audits show requirements met; residual human-error risk is ALARP.
6. Lessons-learned review at project end: design issues, innovative solutions, trade-offs, and improvements to the integration process.

**D. Operate (5.5, pp. 47-49).**
- Follow-up evaluation about one year after start-up (or another agreed interval): a structured meeting with operations staff reviewing O&M level achieved; HFE issues found and changes made or proposed; incidents, near misses and difficulties linked to HFE; lessons to feed back; and the value HFE added, ideally against comparable projects. Preferably specialist-run; on small projects someone HFE-aware with a prompt list. Findings go back to the design team.
- Ongoing HF input areas (Table 6): procedures and work instructions (usability and readability, especially for safety critical tasks); working arrangements (task design, allocation of function, shift patterns and fatigue, handovers, permit to work); staffing and workload studies; risk assessment and incident/near-miss investigation (HEA/SCTA as needed); design support for new or changed equipment; competency management (training needs analysis, competence frameworks).
- Some of these must finish before start-up: procedures for wholly new processes, and staffing/workload studies where staffing must be set.

**E. Decommissioning (5.6, p. 49).** Integrate HFE into decommissioning plans from the start and write an HFIP for each stage, covering procedures, people, organisation, and design of tasks, tools and equipment used for dismantling.

**F. Deliverables (5.7, pp. 49-50).** The HFIP lists them. Possible set: close-out report (minimum for significant-input projects, end of detailed design); HFIR issued with it; HFE plan for construction (large projects); individual study reports (VCA, control room, etc.). Specialists also contribute to design specifications, O&M procedures and work instructions, emergency plans and operational readiness reports; the HFIP should say where.

**G. Annex A screening tools (pp. 51-59).**

Preliminary question set (Table A.1), each yes/no. Does the project or change involve:
1. new plant needing manual operation or local maintenance;
2. new or significantly changed HMIs;
3. systems or equipment new to site, or untested technology;
4. changes to systems heavily dependent on manual interaction (e.g. manual valves);
5. layout changes affecting access or operability;
6. a new or significantly modified control room;
7. new or significantly changed operations requiring revised procedures;
8. implications for safety critical tasks;
9. changes to working environment (temperature, lighting, noise);
10. known HF problems at the facility (incidents, near misses, complaints, absenteeism, injuries);
11. an explicit design objective to use HFE for safety or efficiency?
Record: HFE needed yes/no, and the key HF risk areas.

Level of specialist input and matching strategy (Table A.2, p. 53):
- Low: small changes to simple tasks; little or no scope to influence equipment design (off-the-shelf, like-for-like); requirements covered by existing standards. Strategy: confirm applicable standards and put them in design requirements; check compliance; call a specialist only for non-compliance or trade-offs.
- Medium: tasks complex, lengthy and/or dependent on high human reliability; significant scope to influence new equipment design; a mandated (goal-setting) need to show HFE was applied. Strategy: low plus specialists in design reviews, verification/validation reviews, and hazard identification/risk assessment.
- High: as medium, plus mandated HFE-led activities, specific studies needed (control room, HMI), and safety critical task implications or major accident potential. Strategy: medium plus detailed equipment screening (A.3); appoint a competent person to coordinate; plan HFE-led studies; more specialist involvement and validation; specialist demonstration that safety critical task risks are mitigated.

HFIP decision (Table A.3, p. 54): low = not required, strategy recorded in the project safety plan or equivalent; medium = recommended, may be a section or annex of the safety plan; high = standalone HFIP.

HFE equipment screening tool (A.3, pp. 54-59).
- Purpose: a fast, facilitated review of units and equipment to find issues and opportunities deserving further HFE work; the basis for the HFE strategy.
- When: early, when major units are known or foreseeable but detailed requirements are not specified and vendor contracts are not placed. Intended for high-input projects with a standalone HFIP; simple projects use simpler tools.
- Who: experienced facilitator (technical authority, authorised person or HFE specialist on complex projects; HFE coordinator on simpler ones), O&M representatives, discipline engineers, other specialists. Panel operators attend when instrumentation changes significantly. Record on a shared screen.
- Steps: (1) choose the level: process area/unit, individual equipment item, or operation (turnaround, start-up, ship loading); (2) list the items; (3) rate each against six factors using guide words, noting the reasons; (4) decide per item whether further HFE activity applies; (5) facilitator writes the strategy from the results.
- Six factors and ratings:
  1. Task complexity (Table A.4): Simple (few, discrete, low mental demand, e.g. start/stop buttons, reading gauges) / Moderate / Complex (more than 10 tasks and/or difficult, lengthy, or needing very high reliability) / Unknown. Guide words: operations load, maintenance load, physically demanding, mentally demanding (vigilance, reasoning, trend monitoring), labour intensive, time-consuming.
  2. Unit criticality (Table A.5): Yes / Probably (over 50 % chance that failure hits production or exposes people) / Possibly (under 50 %, not negligible) / No. Guide words: start-up/shutdown, production, product quality, process safety, personnel safety, health, environment, HSE control, sour service (H2S over 10 % = yes, over 1 % = possibly), benzene (same 10 %/1 % split), above auto-ignition temperature, high pressure, high temperature.
  3. Task frequency (Table A.6), excluding routine rounds: Frequent (significant work more than once every 3 months) / Occasional (more than once a year) / Rare (less than once a year) / Unknown. Guide words: start-up/shutdown, trips, routine operations, routine maintenance, breakdown, inspections, cleaning, transportation, resupply.
  4. Novelty (Table A.7): Same / Variant / Similar (new type but consistent with existing skills) / New / Unknown. Guide words: new to asset, business unit, company or industry; capacity; feedstock; process; competencies; procedures; organisation; reliance on contractors.
  5. Design status at screening time.
  6. Known problems with similar equipment or O&M concerns.

## Rules

- Screening cannot be completed without O&M representatives present.
- Choosing how to screen is a judgement needing HFE competence "level 3" (Annex B).
- The facilitator must surface and challenge assumptions about what people will do, how the human role changes, and how users will react.
- More "yes" answers in A.1 suggests more HFE input; none suggests HFE input is unlikely to be needed.
- Staffing studies are triggered by changes to staff numbers, changed roles, or new roles; specialist input recommended.
- All issues closed by end of detailed design; anything left is explicitly carried into construction.

## The Method

This part finishes the screening tables (Annex A), then gives competence and roles (Annex B), the plan template (Annex C), and five of the seven key HFE activities (Annex D.1 to D.5).

**A. Finish HFE screening of each item (Annex A tail, p60-61)**
1. Rate **design scope** (Table A.8): how much can the project still influence the item? Ratings: *A lot* (not off-the-shelf, not yet bought, vendor scope includes design); *A little* (limited influence on the item itself); *Plant layout only* (item fixed, but location, orientation and surrounding space still open); *None* (already bought or wholly off-the-shelf, location frozen).
2. Use the scope guide words to say where influence exists: integral components (valves, flanges, sample points, instruments, labels, signs), plant layout (position, orientation, access, escape, walkways, lay-down), control panels, HMI, instrumentation (incl. alarm set-points), CCTV, lighting, noise and vibration. If there is no opportunity on integral design, further screening may add little.
3. Rate **known problems** (Table A.9): *Major* (significant issues with similar items before), *Minor* (design could have been better), *None*. Guide words: escape routes/congestion, equipment access, awkward or static posture, excessive force/weight, repetitive motion, material handling, poor lighting, weather, comms/noise/radios, confusing or conflicting procedures, mistakes/human errors, other.

**B. Staff the HFE work (Annex B, p62-67)**
4. Pick the competence level each role needs from the three-level framework (see Rules).
5. Allocate HFE responsibilities across operating company, design contractor(s) and vendors, and write the allocation into the HFIP.
6. On larger jobs add an HFE coordinator, an HFE technical authority and, for very large multi-party jobs, an HF working group.

**C. Write the Human Factors Integration Plan (Annex C, p68-69)**
7. Scale detail to the project. Template sections: Introduction (project background, HFE risks, scope of the HFIP, management of the HFIP); HFE integration management (approach overview, constraints, roles and responsibilities, management of HFE issues, HFE deliverables); HFE integration process (standards and guidance applied, end-user involvement, acceptance criteria, review and validation, HFE activities, work programme); References.
8. For each planned HFE activity record: description, approach, resources/inputs/dependencies, acceptance criteria, output/deliverable.
9. Tie the work programme to project milestones and dependencies.

**D. Run the key HFE activities (Annex D, p70-79).** Each is described as: what and when, what it involves, HF specialist level, further reading.

- **D.1 Task requirements analysis (TRA)** (p71-72). A cut-down task analysis aimed at finding design requirements early. When: as early in design as task information allows. Scope: only novel, safety-critical or known-problem equipment, or critical/difficult O&M tasks, where results will matter to design. How: HF-facilitated workshop with equipment designers and O&M end users; walk through tasks (steps, frequency, crew size, duration, complexity), identify safety/production risk from human error, log design requirements. Output: requirements in a standard spreadsheet, feeding HFE and project specifications, vendor ITTs and later validation; actions go to the issues register (HFIR). Requirements are either new (not in existing standards) or existing ones emphasised for critical tasks. Typical content: workspace layout, handling aids for heavy/awkward items, task lighting, PPE or special tools.
- **D.2 Valve criticality analysis (VCA)** (p72-74). Structured way to decide where every valve goes. Phase 1, in Define/FEED: list valves from P&IDs, categorise by agreed criteria (see Rules), mark categories on P&IDs, give layout/piping engineers and vendors location guidance per category. On big jobs categorise by valve type first, handle exceptions, confirm later. Re-do when valves are added or usage changes. Phase 2, FEED through detailed design: compliance review, either inside scheduled 3D model reviews or in dedicated valve workshops. For a non-compliance: confirm intended use, look at the 3D model, find a solution (e.g. temporary access platform, remote actuator), record it and log actions.
- **D.3 Vendor package screening and review** (p75-76). When: early in Define/FEED, once it is known what is bought as packages and what tasks they involve. How: list all packages, screen into two categories (see Rules). Category 1 gets HF review: isometric drawings at minimum, ideally an HF-facilitated workshop with vendor designers and O&M users covering task complexity/criticality/frequency, crew numbers, access and egress, clearances, reach to valves and controls, visibility of gauges. Review the package as installed on the plant, including nearby noise, vibration, heat and dropped-object risk. Log issues; hand design requirements to the vendor.
- **D.4 Control room analysis and design review** (p76-78). Triggered by a new control room, significant change (equipment, furniture, workstations, layout, lighting), transfer of operations, change of function, or centralisation. Also applies to drilling/crane cabins, security centres, operations and emergency rooms. Early workshop (HF facilitator, discipline engineers, control room operators) covering: purpose and operating philosophy in normal and abnormal conditions (incl. legacy systems); functional analysis (e.g. shift handover, permitting, emergency response, personnel monitoring, helicopter/marine management); equipment list; staffing and roles (primary roles need dedicated space, secondary roles only visit); task analysis of key roles incl. safety-critical tasks and workload; link/adjacency analysis for roles and equipment. Then iterate the layout through FEED and detailed design using isometrics and 3D models; mock-up user trials for major builds.
- **D.5 HMI analysis and review** (p78-79). Triggered by new or modified HMIs, mostly DCS, but also significant local interface changes. Off-the-shelf DCS: confirm project requirements, then check the vendor's customised HMI specification. New HMI: requirements analysis workshop with end users (who uses it, critical tasks, screen locations, legacy consistency, conventions for colour, coding, menu hierarchy, navigation). Output: requirements list passed to vendors, sometimes an HMI style guide. Then iterative review of specifications, screenshots and demos; prototype user trials on complex jobs.

## Rules

- **Competence levels (Table B.1, p62-63).** Level 1 HFE support: awareness training from an approved provider, no HF experience needed. Level 2 HFE practitioner: relevant degree or equivalent, graduate membership of a professional HF body, 1 year applying HF in high-hazard industry; can support plans for low/medium complexity projects and produce deliverables. Level 3 HFE lead: degree plus certified professional status, 5+ years in high-hazard industry including at least 2 in energy; can write plans for high-complexity projects, manage specialists and approve deliverables.
- A lower-competence lead may be acceptable where screening shows only low or medium specialist support is needed.
- HFE coordinator: at least Level 1. HFE technical authority: Level 3; holds final sign-off and rules on deviations and dispensations from HFE requirements.
- HFWG is chaired by the HFE lead or delegate, with standing members from disciplines and end users.
- Operating company sets HFE requirements and requires contractors/vendors to show competence and produce their own HFE plans; end users must be available throughout.
- If the contractor changes between FEED and EPC, hand over HFE responsibilities formally.
- HFIP minimum sections: the source marks these in bold, but the bold did not survive extraction (see What Was Skipped).
- **Valve categories.** Cat 1: essential in normal or emergency operation, needs fast unobstructed access (ESD valves, high safety criticality, frequent use). Cat 2: not critical but used in routine operation/maintenance. Cat 3: not critical, rarely used.
- **Valve location guidance.** Cat 1: identity and status visible; permanent access at grade/deck or a permanent elevated standing surface; stairs to a platform if grade is impractical. Cat 2: ideally deck level or stairs; fixed vertical ladders or small platforms only with justification. Cat 3: permanent access desirable not essential; mobile platforms or scaffolding acceptable; portable ladders not to be used. Always leave room for people, tools, parts.
- **Vendor package categories.** Cat 1: critical to operations, safety or environmental integrity, or frequent manual intervention; HF specialist review. Cat 2: infrequent, non-critical intervention; ask vendor for the standards used and the anthropometric basis; no specialist review.
- Minimum Level 2 practitioner to lead TRA, develop valve criteria, review Cat 1 packages, and support control room and HMI analysis. Initial package screening can be done by non-specialists who know the criteria.
- The approach for valve reviews, Cat 1 package reviews and HMI review must be written in the HFIP.
- TRA is not enough for human error assessment, workload evaluation, or training/procedure design; use fuller task analysis there.
- Norwegian sector: NORSOK S-002 requires CRIOP analysis of control rooms (checklist review plus scenario analysis of critical operator actions).

## Mistakes To Avoid

- Running TRA on everything; do it only where it will add real value to design.
- Expecting HF specialists to check every valve on a large project; instead train design engineers to apply the guidelines and raise issues.
- Reviewing a vendor skid in isolation, ignoring where it sits on the plant.

## The Method

**D.6 Alarm system analysis and review (p80-81).** Part of the wider HMI work. Starts in Define/FEED, continues through detailed design.
1. Write an **alarm philosophy**: the system's objectives and how they will be met. It sets rules for categorising and prioritising alarms, acceptable alarm rates and set-points, handling functions (annunciation, acknowledge, reset), shelving, logging, and how alarms look on the HMI (coding, colour) consistent with the rest of the HMI.
2. For new systems, expand it into an **alarm system requirements specification** with detailed design guidance.
3. Identify the alarms needed, including existing ones needing change.
4. **Rationalise**: prioritise each alarm using the philosophy's rules and define the operator response to each. Record in a **master alarm database** kept up to date for the life of the system.
5. Design the system from the rationalisation output and the philosophy/specification.
6. Iteratively review specifications, screenshots and demos with engineers and end users; on complex jobs run prototype trials to judge audible and visual annunciation.

**D.7 Facility/plant layout design review (p81-83).** Starts in Define/FEED and runs through detailed design.
1. Name the applicable ergonomics standards in the HFIP. Where needed, write project HFE design specifications for walkways and emergency egress, elevated platforms, stairs and ladders, lighting, labelling and signage.
2. HF specialists work with design engineers throughout; hold specific meetings for problem areas and document agreed trade-offs.
3. Review 2D general arrangements and isometrics on the desk; walk through 3D models on larger projects.
4. At formal model reviews: **30%** — at minimum HFE reviews the output; this is the key review for space, clearances, walkways and stair locations and the first chance to influence other disciplines. **60%** — HFE-competent people attend; enough detail to check reach to valves and controls and walkway clearances; significant change may still be possible. **90%** — attendance useful; verify earlier issues are closed and do final checks against the HF specification (e.g. visual envelopes).
5. Optionally hold separate HFE-focused model sessions.

**Annex E: HF in hazard identification and risk management (p84-90).** HF belongs in HAZID, HAZOP, bow-tie and other risk assessment. A safety case must show human-failure risk is ALARP. Starts in Define/FEED, continues in detailed design. Most projects need qualitative human error assessment (HEA); some safety cases need quantitative human reliability assessment (HRA) producing human error probabilities (HEPs) for fault and event trees; activities with major accident hazard (MAH) potential need SCTA. Detailed HEA/HRA/safety case methods are stated to be out of scope.

*E.1 HF in HAZOP (p85-88)*
1. Before the HAZOP, the project and HFE lead decide whether HF is formally built in and who covers it; record in the HFIP.
2. HAZOP chair and HF representative meet beforehand to agree approach and guidewords; the representative pre-reads P&IDs and layout drawings.
3. In session: if only causes are being identified, the minimum is adding "human error" as a keyword next to flow, pressure, etc., and recording credible errors; likelihood and mitigation are assessed afterwards by HF specialists.
4. The HF representative contributes like any member: raises known error history, considers error effects on barriers, and challenges unrealistic assumptions about operator performance.
5. After the standard keywords for each node, the representative sums up HF concerns using three guidewords (Table E.1): **operational barriers** (human actions relied on as a barrier), **maintenance barriers** (known cases where maintenance behaviour degraded a safety-critical barrier), **escalation factors** (things at the node that make human unreliability more likely).
6. Afterwards: worksheets and report carry an audit trail of HF issues and assumptions; the representative logs HF actions in the HFIR and brings in specialists if only awareness-level cover was present. Further HEA usually follows.

*E.2 Safety critical task analysis (p88-90).* Eight steps (Figure E.1, adapted from EI guidance):
1. Identify main hazards.
2. Identify and prioritise critical tasks.
3. Understand the tasks.
4. Represent the critical tasks.
5. Identify human failures and performance influencing factors.
6. Determine safety measures to control the failures.
7. Implement and monitor those measures.
8. Review the effectiveness of the process.

Working detail: build a **critical task inventory (CTI)** of tasks affecting MAHs, fed by HAZOPs and other hazard studies; without one, list candidates through talks with discipline engineers and end users and from O&M procedures. Do a high-level screen to drop operations with no safety-critical function, then review the rest for consequence of failure. Collect more task detail if needed. Run an HF-facilitated HEA workshop with end users and discipline engineers: break each task into sub-tasks; for each, take the worst credible consequence of an error or violation; if unrecovered it could affect an MAH, the sub-task is safety critical and is analysed, otherwise dropped. For each critical sub-task record: PIFs/PSFs; likelihood (usually qualitative high/medium/low, matching the project's risk ranking); consequence if unrecovered; existing controls that prevent the failure; existing recovery measures; recommended extra controls/recovery to reach ALARP. Capture in a spreadsheet and in the HFIR.

**Annex F: HFE plan for construction (p91-93).**
1. Write it at the end of detailed design; expand and re-issue at the start of construction once the schedule is known. Standalone on large projects; an annex of the Execute close-out report on simpler ones.
2. Minimum contents: HFE organisation; standards and specifications to apply; awareness training for construction personnel; verification and validation activities; process for logging issues and resolving non-compliances; requirements for any final HFE-in-construction report.
3. Name an HFE lead/HFIM or coordinator for construction. Their duties: manage the plan, deliver awareness training, schedule verification and validation, prepare checklists and do walk-downs and verification reviews, write the final report if required.
4. State who does the checks, their competence, the checklists/tools (can be appendices) and how results are documented. Combine with other O&M surveillance where possible.

## Rules

- Alarm work: minimum Level 2 (HFE practitioner) for the philosophy and requirements specification. Specialist involvement in identification/rationalisation is beneficial not essential, but a specialist review of its output is recommended. At minimum HFE reviews any philosophy produced.
- Layout review: minimum Level 2 practitioner.
- HAZOP minimum: one attendee with at least Level 1 (HFE support) competence made responsible for HF. Where there is significant human interaction and safety-critical tasks, an HF specialist at Level 2 or above attends.
- A task is typically safety critical if a human failure could directly cause a major accident, escalate one, weaken a barrier against one, or impair recovery from one. The exact definition is agreed per project.
- SCTA is required where activities have MAH potential.
- Ordinary HAZOPs are not used to find every human error; convene a dedicated "human HAZOP" or use SCTA for that.
- Human-HAZOP guidewords (Table E.2, Ellis and Holt 2009): No/none = not completed; More/less = too fast/much/long or too slow/little/short; Reverse = wrong direction; Sooner/later = too early, too late, wrong time, wrong order; Part of = partially completed; Other than = wrong object; As well as = wrong task selected or task repeated.
- Where specialist resource is thin, give design engineers awareness training so they can flag issues for specialist review.
- Construction training material is prepared by an HF specialist using the Execute close-out report and carried-forward actions.
- A separate construction report is often unnecessary: documented verification output plus closed issues usually suffices.

## Examples

- Operational barrier issues: operator dismisses a real alarm as a known nuisance alarm; spotting corrosion visually; acting on the wrong one of several similar units.
- Maintenance barrier issues: wrong isolations, wrong gasket on a manifold, valves refitted in the wrong orientation after a turnaround, incorrectly threaded fittings, taking an oil sample from a filter under pressure.
- Escalation factors: tasks heavy on reasoning or memory, cramped access, poor sight lines or lighting, nearby hazards (hot surfaces, height, radiation), PPE/breathing air/winter clothing, unpleasant conditions.
- HAZOP error causes: operator skips a procedure step; operator opens the wrong valve.
- SCTA entries: consequence "tank overfill leading to spill and possible fire"; controls such as supervisory checks or job aids; recovery such as alarms; PIFs such as time pressure or hard-to-reach equipment.
- Field-run items the construction plan must cover: small-bore piping, instrument cabling, secondary cable trays (not normally in the 3D model).

## Mistakes To Avoid

- Skipping HFE at the 30% model review, which makes significant issues at 60% more likely.
- HAZOP teams assuming operators will perform reliably, and so closing hazards with training and procedures when an engineered change is needed.
- Letting field-run items erode designed clearances and sightlines during construction, causing rework.
- Treating the alarm database as a one-off; it must be maintained for the system's life.
- Expecting specialists to review every part of a complex facility.
